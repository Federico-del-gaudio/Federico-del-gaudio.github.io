One Night in Venice

Solo project

Objective: Shooting mechanic, Enemy spawner & power ups

About the Game

A relentless PS1-style bullet hell set in a flooded carnival of screaming masks. Every shot you fire fuels a distorted breakcore soundtrack that surges in volume with your aggression. Survive the nightmare. Dance until you drown in the noise.

In One Night in Venice, your only goal is to shoot and survive waves of ruthless enemies who want nothing but your end. Keep your eyes sharp and your reflexes faster if they catch you, you'll have to start it all over AGAIN. Push yourself to beat your personal record with every run. But above all else: don't stop running.

The Iterative Process

"The First Prototype"

Speedrun · Parry · PS1

Being a Global Game Jam project, I had only 5 days to complete it. I was alone, so I couldn't build complex systems or focus on art and animations. After hours of reflection, three words came to mind: "speedrun," "parry," "PS1."

I prototyped a parry system with static and moving enemies that you had to parry. The idea was to make the player advance by parrying enemies, which would refresh their dash cooldown, gaining advantage and extra time to complete the run as fast as possible.

"Fail and Learn"

Pivoting to a Better Design

I decided to discard the first idea. Unfortunately, time was limited, and making a replayable speedrun game required complex level design that would have caused me to run out of time.

So I integrated shooting into the character, added a timer running in the background, and an enemy chasing the player. And the level design? I noticed the game was fun even in a limited plane. I avoided adding structures to give the player freedom to manage enemy hordes without obstacles.

"Replayability & Key Features"

I wanted my game to be replayable, for the player to improve with each run, but above all, not to get bored while playing.

This meant creating systems that offered variety and challenge each time. I focused on dynamic difficulty scaling, randomized enemy encounters, and meaningful progression that rewarded player skill and strategy.

Enemy Parent

Time constraints meant I couldn't build fully unique behaviors per enemy, so I built one shared behavior for all of them. However, each enemy had different values to diversify the enemy encounter.

Power Up

To keep runs from feeling identical, every time the player collects a gem threshold, they pick 1 of 3 randomized power-ups. This meant different build every run, pushing players to adapt their strategy instead of repeating an optimal path.

Timer & Best Score

To boost replayability, I added a timer and a high-score tracker shown on death, giving beginners a way to measure progress by run duration, and speedrunners a benchmark to beat.

3 Difficulty Modes

Without time for external playtesting, I avoided guessing a single "correct" difficulty and instead split it into three modes: Easy (slower spawn rate to learn patterns), Normal (balanced on my own playtesting), and Hard (double density), letting players find their own difficulty level.

"More Cards? No Problem"

One of the biggest challenges was designing a system that could handle any number of power-up cards without breaking the UI or the game logic.

I created a scalable system that updates values , names , and images in seconds, while also seamlessly updating the in-game UI.

"The Power up problem"

When selecting a power-up, the game pauses; once a choice is made, pacing resumes as before.

However, the transition from the selection screen back to active gameplay felt too snap, so I gave the player a brief advantage, slowing enemies by 70% for 0.35 seconds giving the player time to react

Implementation Insights - Enemy

Key Learnings from Enemy Movement Implementation

In One Night in Venice enemies kill you in a single hit. The main challenge was creating enemies that posed a real threat without being frustrating.

Having them simply rush the player in a straight line felt cheap. After brainstorming, I decided to make the enemies orbit around the player, gradually closing the distance. This dynamic allows players to "tame" the horde through smart movement.

I implemented this behavior using mathematical calculations playing with sine waves (sin) to achieve that smooth orbiting and "blob-like" crowd behavior.

Additionally, I ensured that enemies always dynamically look at the player based on their relative position.

Progressive Difficulty Design

One of the key features of One Night in Venice was the roguelike power-up card system. Throughout each run, after collecting a certain number of gems, players could choose to boost stats like damage, bullet speed, or movement speed.

This progression was crucial because the game became scaled in difficulty not by making enemies stronger, but by drastically increasing spawn quantities.

Structurally, I created a system using Data Tables to link the card data directly to the corresponding gameplay variables and connected them to the UI.

I then implemented a shuffle algorithm to ensure that the card selection was completely randomized upon each level-up.

Implementation Insights - Enemy feedbacks

Hit stop and flash hit effect

Game feedback is absolutely vital; elements like audio cues, screen shake, and haptics drastically increase the richness of the player experience. My goal was to maximize this impact.

Beyond adding heavy hit sounds and enemy audio, I focused on implementing the classic retro "white flash" hit effect, inspired by games like Metal Slug and Cuphead. Technically, I achieved this in Unreal Engine by creating a custom white material flash that triggers dynamically.

Using Blueprints, I utilized a Timeline to handle the jitter and duration, swapping the enemy's material instantly whenever a bullet registers a hit.
